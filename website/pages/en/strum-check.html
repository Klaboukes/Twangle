Tools · Tuning

Strum check

Strum all the open strings once. Every string shows at the same time which way to turn it. There is nothing to switch on: the tuner hears a strum by itself.

Check every string at once

  1. On the tuner, strum all the open strings, without pressing any, and let them ring.
  2. After about half a second a mark stands above each string's button: an arrow up for a string to tighten, an arrow down for one to loosen, a check mark for one in tune, or a dash when Twangle didn't hear it.
  3. Under the meter it says how many are in tune, for example 4 of 6 in tune, or All strings in tune.

Then tune the ones that are off

Just play a string that is off and tune it as always: the meter shows how far, the word which way. Its mark follows: once the string is in tune it gets its check mark, and the count goes up, 5 of 6 in tune. The other marks stay until you have tuned them too, so you always see which strings are left.

Strum again any time to check the whole instrument once more. It is the quickest way to see whether your instrument is still in tune between songs, or which string slipped.

Good to know

  • Strum firmly and let the strings ring: the first, loud moment of a strum counts most.
  • A paler mark is less sure. In standard tuning that is often the B and the high E: their notes overlap with the low E's harmonics, so Twangle can't hear them as clearly. If one looks off, play it on its own: its mark follows the tuner.
  • A strum counts a string as in tune by the same rule as the tuner: within 5 cents, or 2 when Settings → In-tune precision is set to Precise.
  • Plucking one string at a time is no strum: the tuner simply reads that string. A fretted chord is no strum of the open strings either: Twangle names it instead (see Chord name).
  • The marks go after half a minute without a sound, and when you pick another tuning.
  • It works with your tuning, whatever it is, on every instrument but the 12-string guitar (its twelve strings ring too close together to tell apart), with the instrument's strings: not in Any note.
For the curious: hearing six strings at once

First Twangle has to know a strum when it hears one. When a sound starts suddenly, it looks at its first moments: a strum of the open strings is at least three notes, each one of your open strings, each one struck just now (louder than it rang a moment before). One string plucked while others ring is not, nor is a voice or a knock, so the tuner just goes on.

A single string is easy: its wave repeats, and the time it takes gives the note. In a strum all the waves are on top of each other. But a string's sound is more than one note: it is a row of harmonics at one, two, three, four times its pitch. Twangle knows which strings your tuning has, so for each string it looks in the sound's spectrum for that row of harmonics near where the string should be, and measures exactly where they are. It does this on about a third of a second of sound, ten times a second while the strings ring, and weighs the readings by how loud they were.

Some harmonics of different strings land on almost the same pitch. In standard tuning the B string is almost exactly three times the low E's pitch, and the high E four times, so every harmonic of theirs is also one of the low E's. Where two strings share a harmonic, Twangle leans on the ones that don't, and when none are left, it reads the shared one and marks the string less sure.
