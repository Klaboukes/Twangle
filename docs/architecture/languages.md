## Languages and registers
**English (default), Dutch, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese (Brazil), Chinese (Simplified), Japanese, Korean, Hindi, Indonesian, Russian, Turkish, Polish, Arabic, Vietnamese, Thai, Chinese (Traditional), Bengali, Ukrainian, Swedish, Norwegian (Bokmål), Danish, Finnish, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Swahili, Amharic, Romanian, Czech and Greek.** Register: German, Spanish, Italian and Dutch use the informal you (du, tú, tu, je), French uses vous, Brazilian Portuguese você. French puts a no-break space (U+00A0) before : and ? and inside « ». Brazilian Portuguese calls the guitar "Violão" ("guitarra" is the electric one). Spanish is one folder (`values-es`, written for Spain with tú) that every Spanish phone shows, Latin America's too (Android falls back from `es-MX` or `es-419` to `es`); Play's listing has it twice, `es-ES` and `es-419` with the Latin American words (*celular*), since Google doesn't say which listing a Latin American phone gets (docs/store-listing.md, *Folders*). Chinese uses 你, Japanese polite です/ます for sentences and short plain labels, Korean 해요체, Hindi आप with the loanwords Indian guitarists use (ट्यूनर, ट्यूनिंग, स्ट्रम), Indonesian Anda and the guitarist's *stem* (tuning), *genjreng* (strum), *kres*/*mol* (♯/♭). Chinese says a sharp before the letter (升C) and reads 2/4 as 四二拍. Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Indonesian have only the plural quantity "other"; Hindi has "one" and "other". Russian and Polish use the informal ты/ty and their guitarist words (тюнер, строй, подтяни/ослабь; stroik, strój, podciągnij/popuść); Turkish uses the short sen imperative (akort, tel, yükselt/düşür). Russian and Polish plurals have one/few/many/other (1 цент, 2 цента, 5 центов, "other" for fractions; 1 cent, 2 centy, 5 centów, 12 centów), Turkish one/other with the noun in the singular (12 sent). Russian writes Гц for Hz. Text set in capitals in code uses the app's locale (`uppercase(LocalConfiguration.current.locales[0])`), so Turkish i becomes İ; Kotlin's plain `uppercase()` is locale-free.md), *Visual styles*, **Lettering**). No style font covers Han, Kana, Hangul or Devanagari, so those glyphs come from the phone's own fonts (Android falls back per glyph), without the styles' extra letter spacing. Indonesian's folder is `values-in` (Android's old code; a phone set to `id` resolves to it). Arabic is Modern Standard Arabic with the masculine imperative of app texts (اعزف وترًا, اختر وترًا) and the words Arab guitarists use: الموالف (tuner), الدوزان (tuning), وتر/أوتار (string/strings), شُدّ/أرخِ (tune up/down), دييز/بيمول (♯/♭ spoken), اضرب (strum); Drop D, Open G and Low G stay in Latin letters, like Russian. Its plurals use all six quantities (zero, one, two, few, many, other: ٠ سنت, بسنت واحد, بسنتين, ٣ سنتات, ١١ سنتًا, ١٠٠ سنت). No style font has Arabic, so it comes from the phone's font. Each module keeps its texts in `res/values/strings.xml` with a copy per language in `res/values-<language>/strings.xml`; brand and symbols (`app_name`, `DADGAD`, ♯/♭, ♪) are `translatable="false"`. Vietnamese uses the neutral *bạn* and bare imperatives (Gảy một dây, Chọn một dây), *Hãy …* only to open a full-sentence request, and the guitarist's words *lên dây* (tune, the tuner), *kiểu lên dây* (tuning), *dây buông* (open string), *quét* (strum), *phím* (fret), *căng lên*/*chùng xuống* (tune up/down), *thăng*/*giáng* (♯/♭ spoken); Chromatic, instrument names and Drop D, Open G stay in Latin letters as Vietnamese players write them, and "Standard" is *Tiêu chuẩn* so it doesn't clash with *chuẩn* (in tune). Vietnamese has only the plural quantity "other" (12 cent, 3 dây) and writes numbers with a decimal comma (440,0 Hz). Its note names are letters with sharps (guitarists read chord sheets and tuners in letters, though schools teach Đô Rê Mi); TalkBack says solfège in Vietnamese spelling (`spoken_solfege` Đô Rê Mi Fa Son La Si, so "Do" isn't read "zô"). Thai uses a neutral, polite register with no pronoun where it can (plain imperatives: เล่นสักสาย, เลือกสาย), คุณ where "you" is needed and no ครับ/ค่ะ particles, as Thai apps do, and the loanwords Thai players use: จูนเนอร์ (tuner), จูนขึ้น/จูนลง (tune up/down), การตั้งสาย (tuning), สายเปล่า (open string), เฟรต, ตี (strum), อ็อกเทฟ (octave as a register; interval names stay คู่ 8), คาโป้, ฟอร์ม (chord shape), and chord and scale qualities in Thai letters (เมเจอร์, ไมเนอร์, เพนทาโทนิก); Drop D, Open G and Low G stay in Latin letters, like Russian. Thai has only the plural quantity "other" (12 เซนต์) and Android writes its numbers in Western digits (Thai digits ๐–๙ only with `-u-nu-thai`), so the texts do too. Thai puts no spaces between words: a space ends a phrase where English has a comma or full stop, and there are no full stops; Android's line breaker has a Thai dictionary, but loanwords in Thai letters are not in it and break mid-word, and the fit checks accept a break only at a space, hyphen or zero-width space. So a loanword that can wrap gets a word joiner (U+2060) before each syllable inside it, and a compound a zero-width space (U+200B) between its parts (the scale names, ไมเนอร์\u200Bเพนทาโทนิก; Settings' บาโรก). Its note names are letters with sharps (Thai guitarists write C, C#, Am; โด เร มี is for school singing). No style font has Thai, so it comes from the phone's font (`OTHER_SCRIPT_LANGUAGES`). Font and library names are proper names and stay in English. Traditional Chinese (`b+zh+Hant`: one folder for Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau, since Android resolves a zh-TW or zh-HK phone to it; written for Taiwan) uses 你 and Taiwan's words (設定, 螢幕, 預設, 應用程式, 輕觸 for tap, 震動, 訊號, 公釐) and Taiwanese guitarists' terms: 格 for fret (第 5 格), 刷弦 for strum, 移調夾 (capo), 按法 (chord shape), 封閉 (barre), 下弦枕 (saddle), 調音 for a tuning (not the Mainland's 定弦; Drop D, Open G stay in Latin letters), 藍調, 完全四度/五度/八度, 烏克麗麗, 斑鳩琴, 左撇子模式 (left-handed), 「」 quotes. Like Simplified Chinese it says a sharp before the letter (升C), but TalkBack reads time signatures as 四分之三拍. Plural quantity "other" only, Western digits, note names letters with sharps (`forLanguage` gives every `zh` DEFAULT). No style font has Han, so it comes from the phone's font (`OTHER_SCRIPT_LANGUAGES` holds `zh` for both scripts), and the language's `localeList` gives the Traditional glyphs. Play has no Traditional Chinese listing, only Taiwan's and Hong Kong's, so its store texts are in two folders, `zh-TW` and `zh-HK` (the same texts with Hong Kong's 結他 for guitar, 網絡, 帳戶; docs/store-listing.md, *Folders*). In the app a Chinese language is matched by its script, so a zh-TW app locale shows 繁體中文 as picked (`AppLanguages.selectedOf`). Bengali (`bn`, one folder for Bangladesh and West Bengal, which share the written standard; Play folder `bn-BD`) uses আপনি with the polite imperative of standard colloquial Bengali (বাজান, বেছে নিন, টিউন করুন), as Google's and most Bengali apps do, and the loanwords Bengali guitarists use: টিউনার, টিউনিং, স্ট্রাম, কর্ড, ফ্রেট, ক্যাপো, তার for string, সুর চড়ান/সুর নামান (tune up/down), স্কেল for a song's key; time signatures are said the South Asian way, "x by y" in English number words (থ্রি বাই ফোর তাল). Its plurals have one and other with the same noun (১ সেন্ট, ৫ সেন্ট), counted things take the classifier টি (৩টি তার), and sentences end with the দাঁড়ি ।. Android formats Bengali numbers in Bengali digits (০–৯, CLDR's default for `bn`), so `localDigits` draws them on the meter, tempo dial and beat numbers, and the literal numbers in its texts are Bengali digits too (৪৪০ হার্জ, ১২তম ফ্রেট); note names and their octaves (A4, DADGAD) and the interval signs (m2 … P8) stay Latin. Its note names are letters with sharps (Western guitar is learnt from English chord charts; sargam is movable). No style font has Bengali, so it comes from the phone's font (`OTHER_SCRIPT_LANGUAGES`). Ukrainian (`uk`, Play folder `uk`) uses the informal ти, like Russian and Polish, and the words Ukrainian guitarists use: тюнер, стрій (строю, строї) for a tuning, підтягни/послаб (tune up/down), лад (fret), аплікатура (chord shape), тоніка (root), каподастр, сідло (saddle), Торкнися for tap, додаток for app; it is real Ukrainian, not Russian forms (Налаштування, Гами, Тренування слуху, Для шульги), and Drop D, Open G, Low G stay in Latin letters, like Russian. Its plurals have one/few/many/other (1 цент, 2 центи, 5 центів, "other" for fractions: 1,5 цента); it writes Гц for Hz, «» quotes and the apostrophe as ’ (U+2019, п’ять), so no `\'` is needed. The metronome's Listen line says До налаштувань ("to settings") where the tuner and Ear training say Відкрити налаштування: beside Мікрофон вимкнено at 2× text, on a phone on its side, the longer one ran past the line even at its smallest size (overnight run of 2026-10-30). Android writes its numbers with Western digits and a decimal comma (110,0 Гц). Its note names are German letters with H, like Russian and Polish (Ukrainian music schools and chord books write H7, and B for B♭; younger players who learn from English material often write B7, so the habit is mixed and H is the more common), and TalkBack says them as Russian's are (no `speech.xml`; дієз/бемоль). Swedish, Norwegian Bokmål and Danish use the informal du and short imperatives (Spela en sträng, Spill en streng, Spil en streng), as apps there do; Swedish and Danish TalkBack click labels are infinitives, after Android's "för att …"/"for at …" (Byta instrument eller stämning, Låse til denne streng). Their guitarist words: Swedish stämmare (tuner), stämning, lösa strängar, band (fret, 12:e bandet), slå an (strum), Tap tempo, ”…” quotes; Norwegian stemmer (tuner), stemming, bånd (12. bånd), slå an, sal (saddle), « » quotes; Danish Tuner (the guitarist's word; stemmeapparat only once in the store text, for search), stemning, løse strenge, bånd (12. bånd), Kammertone for A4, » « quotes. All three have one/other plurals with "cent" unchanged (1 cent, 12 cent) and a decimal comma (440,0 Hz). Note names follow what is most common in each country (owner's rule): Swedish letters with B (Swedish publishers and teaching moved from H to B in the 1980s and 1990s), Norwegian and Danish German letters with H (guitar guides write E-A-D-G-H-E, "H-strengen"; B is spreading among younger players), so `forLanguage` gives `GERMAN` for `da`, `nb`, `nn` and `no`, and their four B tunings are named to match (H-standard, Drop H, B-standard and Drop B for the B♭ ones). TalkBack says Norwegian and Danish names the German way (`speech.xml` `german`: ciss/cis, ess/es, b, h), and Swedish its own way (`swedish`: the Dutch forms with the s doubled, ciss, fiss, dess, ess, ass, bess, because a Swedish voice reads the Dutch one-s forms with a long vowel and "fis", "as" and "dis" are Swedish words). Swedish's long scale names have a zero-width space between their parts (`%1$s moll​pentatonisk`, `dur​pentatonisk`), so on a narrow phone at 2× text they break there, never inside a part (overnight run of 2026-10-30, Futuristic and Space Age). Norwegian's folder is `values-nb` (Bokmål, which Android's own language list sets); Play's Norwegian listing is `no-NO` (`StoreListingTest`'s `PLAY_FOLDERS`; the store screenshots set the app to `nb-NO` for it). Android's resource lookup has no alias from `no` or `nn` to `nb`, so a phone whose only language is plain Norwegian (`no`, some old or custom systems) or Nynorsk (`nn`) gets English texts (checked on the phone, 2026-10-29); one that lists Bokmål after Nynorsk gets Bokmål. Android puts the locale whose texts it found first in the configuration (`nb` there, English's for a lone `no`), so `appLanguage()` always matches the texts on screen; `forLanguage` maps `no` and `nn` to H too, only as a safeguard. Covering them would take a locale wrapper in the app, the widget and the watch that turns `no`/`nn` into `nb`, not worth it while Android itself offers only Bokmål. Finnish uses the informal sinä and its short imperative (Soita kieli, Valitse kieli, Napauta), as modern Finnish apps do, with the guitarist's words viritin (tuner), vireessä (in tune), kiristä/löysää (tune up/down), kieli (string), nauha (fret, 12. nauha), talla (saddle), ote (chord shape), kapo, lyönti (one strum; Lyöntitesti), ” ” quotes. Chord and scale names put the root before a hyphen (A-duuri, A-molli, H-septimisointu), and every other placeholder stands after a colon or in the nominative, so no case ending ever lands on a placeholder; "Twangle" is inflected only where Finnish must (Tietoja Twanglesta). Plurals are one/other with the partitive above one (1 sentti, 12 senttiä; the cents pill says "1 sentin matalalla", "12 senttiä matalalla"), and the decimal comma (440,0 Hz). Note names are German letters with H (Finnish school music, songbooks and chord charts: H7, Hm; B is B♭), so `forLanguage` gives `GERMAN` for `fi`, the B tunings are H-standardi, Drop H, B-standardi and Drop B, and TalkBack says them the German way (`speech.xml` `german`: cis, es, b, h). ä ö å (and š ž in loanwords) are in every face. Finnish words run long (Kromaattinen, Duuripentatoninen, C♯-duuriseptimisointu) and break only at hyphens, so the fit checks matter most for it. Its two pentatonic scale names have a zero-width space between their parts, as Swedish's do (`%1$s-molli​pentatoninen`, `duuri​pentatoninen`): at 2× text on a narrow phone in Futuristic and Space Age the name broke inside "mollipentatoninen" even at its smallest size (overnight run of 2026-10-31). Tamil (`ta`, one folder for India, Sri Lanka and Singapore; Play folder `ta-IN`) uses the polite -வும் imperative of Android's own Tamil (தட்டவும், மீட்டவும், ட்யூன் செய்யவும்) for instructions and tips, the bare stem for short buttons as Android's buttons do (சேமி, மூடு, ரத்துசெய், அனுமதி; மீண்டும் முயல் on the watch, while the phone keeps Android's மீண்டும் முயலவும், which its error texts quote), the infinitive for TalkBack actions said after "Double-tap to" (பூட்ட, திறக்க, ஒலிக்கச் செய்ய), noun phrases for switches (சுருதி சரியானதும் அதிர்வு), நீங்கள் where "you" is needed, and -ங்கள் in the store texts, which address the reader. Its guitarist words are the loanwords Tamil players use: ட்யூனர், ட்யூனிங், ஸ்ட்ரம், கார்டு (chord), ஸ்கேல், ஃப்ரெட் (12-ஆவது ஃப்ரெட்), கேப்போ, ஷேப், ரூட், சேடில், நெக், கீ for a song's key (the peg is பெக், never கீ), the chord and scale qualities in Tamil letters (மேஜர், மைனர், செவன்த், சஸ் 2, பென்டடோனிக்), Drop D and Open G as டிராப் D, ஓப்பன் G; and Tamil words where they are plain: கம்பி for string (திறந்த கம்பி, open string), இசைக்கருவி for instrument (கருவி alone is a tool), சுருதி சரி for in tune, இறுக்கவும்/தளர்த்தவும் (tighten/loosen), சென்ட் குறைவு/அதிகம் (cents flat/sharp), தாழ்வு/உயர்வு (Any note's too low/high), பயிற்சி (Practice), செவிப் பயிற்சி (Ear training), சுருதிக் கோடு (Pitch trail); time signatures are read "three-four" in Tamil numbers (மூன்று-நான்கு தாளம்). Its plurals have one and other, mostly with the same noun after a number (1 சென்ட், 12 சென்ட்), and a case ending after a number or placeholder takes a hyphen (5-இல், %1$s-ஐ). Android writes Tamil numbers in Western digits (CLDR's default for `ta` is `latn`; Tamil digits ௦–௯ only with `-u-nu-tamldec`), so the texts do too. Its note names are letters with sharps (Western guitar is learnt from chord charts in letters; Carnatic sa ri ga is movable), so `forLanguage` gives it DEFAULT, and TalkBack says the letters with ஷார்ப்/ஃப்ளாட் (no `speech.xml`). Tamil's script comes from the phone's font. Tamil words are wide, so the metronome's Listen line uses one-word actions, மீண்டும் ("again") and அமைப்புகள் ("settings"), where the tuner says மீண்டும் முயலவும் and அமைப்புகளைத் திற, and Practice's "Last used" is கடந்த முறை ("last time"): at 2× text the longer ones ran past their box or broke inside a word (fit checks of 2026-11-08). Telugu (`te`, one folder for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana; Play folder `te-IN`) uses మీరు with the polite -ండి imperative of Android's own Telugu (మీటండి, ఎంచుకోండి, ట్యాప్ చేయండి) for instructions and tips, the bare imperative for short buttons as Android's buttons do (సేవ్ చేయి, రద్దు చేయి, తొలగించు, మూసివేయి, అనుమతించు; మళ్లీ ప్రయత్నించు on the watch, while the phone keeps Android's మళ్లీ ప్రయత్నించండి, which its error texts quote in “ ”), and verbal nouns in -డం for TalkBack actions said after "Double-tap to" (ఈ తీగకు లాక్ చేయడం, వాయిద్యం లేదా ట్యూనింగ్ మార్చడం). Its guitarist words are the loanwords Telugu players use: ట్యూనర్, ట్యూనింగ్, స్ట్రమ్, కార్డ్ (chord), స్కేల్, ఫ్రెట్ (12వ ఫ్రెట్), కపో, షేప్, రూట్, సాడిల్, నెక్, కీ for a song's key (the peg is పెగ్), ఓపెన్ for an open string (ఓపెన్ తీగలు), the chord and scale qualities in Telugu letters (మేజర్, మైనర్, సెవెన్త్, సస్ 2, పెంటాటోనిక్), Drop D and Open G as డ్రాప్ D, ఓపెన్ G, and time signatures in English number words (త్రీ-ఫోర్ టైమ్; తాళం would suggest a Carnatic tala); loanword plurals take the Telugu -లు (కార్డ్‌లు, స్కేల్‌లు, కంట్రోల్‌లు, never -స్); and Telugu words where they are plain: తీగ for string, వాయిద్యం for instrument, శ్రుతి సరి for in tune, బిగించండి/సడలించండి (tighten/loosen), సెంట్లు తక్కువ/ఎక్కువ (cents flat/sharp), మరీ తక్కువ/మరీ ఎక్కువ (Any note's too low/high), సాధన (Practice), చెవి శిక్షణ (Ear training), శ్రుతి రేఖ (Pitch trail), వేగ సాధన (Speed trainer), తంబూరా (tanpura). Its plurals have one and other (1 సెంట్, 12 సెంట్లు), and a case ending is written straight onto a number, a placeholder or a Latin word (6లో, %2$dలో, Twangleకు, Google Playలో). Android writes Telugu numbers in Western digits (CLDR's default for `te` is `latn`; Telugu digits ౦–౯ only with `-u-nu-telu`), so the texts do too. Its note names are letters with sharps (Western guitar is learnt from chord charts in letters; Carnatic sa ri ga is movable), so `forLanguage` gives it DEFAULT, and TalkBack says the letters with షార్ప్/ఫ్లాట్ (no `speech.xml`). Telugu's script comes from the phone's font. Like Tamil's, the metronome's Listen line uses one-word actions, మళ్లీ ("again") and సెట్టింగ్‌లు ("settings"), beside మైక్రోఫోన్ ఆఫ్. ## Day and night
Owner, 2026-10-28: with 25 languages, translating every new or reworded text into all of them would slow every day's work. So the work is split:
- **By day**, a change that adds or rewords a text writes it in English and Dutch (`twangle.dayLanguages=en,nl` in `gradle.properties`; Dutch because the test phone runs Twangle in Dutch and the website is English and Dutch). The daytime UI tests check the same two (`scripts/ui-tests.sh --languages` reads the list). Every new English text gets a translator note, an XML comment right above it in `res/values/strings.xml` saying what it means and where it shows (`<!-- Under the meter when the string is in tune. -->`), because the night's translators see only the text and the note.
- **In the full check**, its first step (the `/translate` skill, also run by hand at any time) translates whatever the other languages lack or have stale, in parallel batches of languages, from the English, its note, the Dutch, the register line above and the [glossary](glossary.md), and lands it through its own PR before that run's `@EveryLanguage` tests, which then check the new texts on the phone.
- **The record**, `translations/<language>.txt`, one per translated language: one line per text, `<module> <name> <hash>`, the hash being the first 8 hex digits of SHA-256 over the English the translation was made from (whitespace runs made one space; a plural as one `quantity=text` line per item). A reworded English text no longer matches, so its translations show up as **stale**, not only new texts as **missing**. Notes don't count, so rewording a note changes nothing. `python -I scripts/translations.py status [--json]` lists what is missing or stale per language (with `--json`: the English, the note, the Dutch and the current translation of each); `scripts/translations.py mark <language…|all>` writes the record from today's English after a translation. Dutch is in the record like the rest: by day its texts are written but not marked, so at night `/translate` checks each new or reworded Dutch text against its English, fixes it if it wasn't reworded, and marks it.
- **Two modes** of `LocalizationTest` (*Guard*): by day English and Dutch must be complete and the rest may lag; strict (`-Ptwangle.strictTranslations=true`) wants every listed language complete and matching the record. Lint's `MissingTranslation` is a warning by day (root `build.gradle.kts`) and an error in strict builds.
- **The gate:** a release builds only from a tree that passes strict. The release skill's first step and `scripts/release.sh bundle` run `./gradlew :app:testDebugUnitTest --tests '*LocalizationTest' -Ptwangle.strictTranslations=true` and stop if any language is incomplete or stale; the full check has translated them by then, and a release already waits for a clean full check.
- A text removed from English is removed from every language in the same change (mechanical, no translating); a text English lacks fails by day too, as does lint's `ExtraTranslation`.

## In-app language (Settings → Language)
the same choice as phone Settings → Apps → Twangle → Language, made inside the app, on every Android version. `SettingsScreen` reads and sets it with AppCompat's `AppCompatDelegate.getApplicationLocales` / `setApplicationLocales`: on Android 13+ that is the system's per-app language (so both places always agree), on Android 8–12 AppCompat keeps it in a file of its own (`autoStoreLocales` on `AppLocalesMetadataHolderService` in `:app`'s manifest) and sets it on the activity, which is why `MainActivity` is an `AppCompatActivity` (its window theme `Theme.Twangle` derives from `Theme.AppCompat.Light.NoActionBar`, and `Theme.AppCompat.NoActionBar` in dark mode; Compose draws everything, so nothing else of AppCompat shows). The languages come from `BuildConfig.LANGUAGES`, which `:app`'s build file fills from `twangle.languages` like `localeFilters`, so the setting offers exactly the system list (`LocalizationTest` checks the build file). `AppLanguages` (`:feature:settings`, JVM-tested in `AppLanguagesTest`) turns qualifiers into language tags (`pt-rBR` → `pt-BR`), names each language in its own words with a capital ("Polski", "Português (Brasil)"; Chinese by its script, 简体中文, as Android's own list does) and matches a regional variant picked in phone Settings to its language. Names are not translated, and each carries its language (a `SpanStyle` `localeList`, which reaches TalkBack as a `LocaleSpan`), so TalkBack reads "Polski" in Polish and Han characters get their Chinese or Japanese forms. A switch recreates the activity; texts, digits and the note-name default (`appLanguage()`, from the configuration) follow at once, and Settings stays open. The home-screen widget is redrawn in the new language (`onLanguageChange` → `TwangleApplication.redrawWidgets`; Android 13+ also through `onConfigurationChanged`); on Android 8–12 it takes the language from AppCompat (`TuningWidget.inAppLanguage`), so in a process started for the widget alone, before the app has been opened since the phone started, it shows the phone's language until then. The Play Store bundle doesn't split by language (`bundle.language.enableSplit = false`), so every language is installed and one the phone doesn't use works at once. The watch app has no setting and follows the watch's language.

## Per-app language (Android 13+)
`:app` sets `androidResources.generateLocaleConfig = true` with `res/resources.properties` (`unqualifiedResLocale=en`). Both `:app` and `:wear` set `localeFilters` from `twangle.languages`, so the generated locale config (the in-app language list) holds exactly the listed languages, and library texts never appear in an unlisted one. The locale config takes a language from any resource, libraries included (Compose and AndroidX ship most languages), so a listed language shows up in the list even before Twangle's own texts exist; the guard below is what keeps the list honest.

## Formatting
plurals for counts (`tuner_cents_flat/sharp`) and placeholders for numbers (`%1$.1f Hz`), so Dutch shows "440,0 Hz". **Digits** follow the app's language as Android's resources format them (`String.format` in the app locale): Arabic gets Arabic-Indic digits (٤٤٠ هرتز), Bengali Bengali digits (৪৪০ হার্জ), Marathi Devanagari digits (४४० Hz). Numbers the app draws itself (the metronome's tempo and its ruler, the time signatures ٣/٤, the watch's "+١٢") go through `localDigits` (`:core:designsystem`), so every number on a screen uses the same digits; literal numbers in an Arabic text are written ٠–٩ too, in a Bengali one ০–৯, in a Marathi one ०–९. Note names keep their octave in 0–9 (A4, E♭2): they are Latin symbols, and a Western digit after a letter stays in the letter's left-to-right run (Unicode bidi rule W7), so "E♭2" can't come apart inside Arabic text, where an Arabic-Indic one would move the ♭ in front. Note names are not translated; they follow the notation setting, whose default follows the language ([Notes, tunings and audio](notes-and-audio.md), **Notation by language**). Chinese, Japanese and Korean text may break between any two characters, so a one-word label in a width-sharing row (the mode switch) is measured at its full one-line width, not its narrowest.

## Right to left (Arabic)
both manifests set `android:supportsRtl="true"`, so Compose mirrors the layout: texts, rows, settings, panels, the navigation bar and rail, the meter picker, switches; the back arrow and the settings chevrons are `autoMirrored` vector drawables. What stays left to right, after Material's rule not to mirror graphs, gauges and clocks, and because low pitch sits left like a keyboard and a stave: the meter (flat left, sharp right; drawn on a `Canvas`, whose coordinates never mirror), the wave, the string row and a strum's marks over it and the style cards' little strings (`CompositionLocalProvider(LocalLayoutDirection provides Ltr)`), the metronome's tempo with − slower left and + faster right and its ruler (slower to the left, like the A4 slider), the beat lights (counted left to right, as in sheet music), the A4 slider with its − and + (a pitch scale), the tuning sheet's "Lower by" − amount + (as every stepper), and the tuning editor's − note + group. A note name with its octave (the big note, the watch's) is also wrapped left to right, so the octave stays right of the letter. Compose sets a paragraph's direction from the layout, not from its text, so a list of note names in a right-to-left screen is given `TextDirection.Ltr` (the panel's open strings, else "E♭ … E♭" puts its last ♭ in front). The play ▶ icon isn't mirrored (Material: media controls keep their direction).

## Guard
`LocalizationTest` (JVM, `:app`; the translation part is `TranslationCheck`, tested on a made-up tree in `TranslationCheckTest`) reads `twangle.languages` and `twangle.dayLanguages` and fails when:
- a module's English string has no copy in a day language (English, Dutch), or any copy's placeholders differ from the English (for plurals: the "other" item must match, every other item may only use placeholders of "other", and only Android's quantities are allowed), or a language has a string English doesn't;
- in strict mode (`-Ptwangle.strictTranslations=true`, *Day and night*): any listed language lacks a string, has no record, or holds a translation whose record hash isn't today's English, or the record lists a text English no longer has;
- the day languages don't start with the default language, aren't all listed, or `scripts/ui-tests.sh` doesn't read them;
- a `values-<x>/strings.xml` exists for a language that isn't listed;
- the first language isn't `unqualifiedResLocale`, or an app module doesn't take `localeFilters` from the list;
- Kotlin passes a hard-coded literal to `Text(...)`, `contentDescription`, `stateDescription`, `onClickLabel` and similar.

`StoreListingTest` (JVM, `:app`) holds the Play Store texts in `fastlane/metadata/android/<Play locale>/` to the same list: one folder per language, each with a title, short and full description within Play's limits ([store listing](../store-listing.md), *The texts, ready to paste*).

Every module's `res` and Kotlin sources, the build files, `gradle.properties`, the record in `translations/`, `scripts/ui-tests.sh`, the strict flag and the store texts are declared inputs of `:app`'s unit tests, so changing any of them reruns the test. Lint's `MissingTranslation` (a warning by day) and `StringFormat*` back it up.

## Adding a language
append its qualifier to `twangle.languages`, then add `res/values-<language>/strings.xml` to every module that has a `res/values/strings.xml` (today `:app`, `:wear`, the four features and `:core:designsystem`), translating every translatable string and plural. `LocalizationTest` names what is missing. Then write its record (`python -I scripts/translations.py mark <language>`) and its section of the [glossary](glossary.md). Add its Play Store texts too, `fastlane/metadata/android/<Play locale>/` with `title.txt`, `short_description.txt` and `full_description.txt` translated from `en-US`; `StoreListingTest` names a missing folder. Nothing else changes: the language list on the phone and the watch's texts follow. The `/add-language` skill walks through it, including the note-name default, TalkBack names, fonts and right-to-left checks.
