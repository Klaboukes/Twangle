Drone sounds

Four candidates for the steady low note the Scales tool will play under a scale. Each plays the root (D) alone, with its fifth, with its fifth and octave, a fifth lower, and under a scale on the guitar, then stops. Listen on the phone's speaker and in headphones: which one (or which two) should the app have, and should the fifth and the octave be offered?

Cello the tuner's own cello voice

A bowed cello holding its open D: warm and woody, with the bow's soft hiss and a gentle sway, as a player would. Dark, so the phone's speaker plays little of it.

Soft organ

Quiet church-organ pipes: smooth, round and perfectly still. Nothing moves, so any out-of-tune note played over it is easy to hear as a slow beat.

Tanpura an Indian drone

A bright, buzzing hum whose overtones slowly sweep up and down, a shimmer that never quite repeats. The richest of the four, and the one the phone's speaker plays best.

Shruti box a singer's drone

A small hand-pumped reed box, like a harmonium: reedy and a little nasal, gently breathing with the bellows, with a touch of air.

All twenty are about equally loud by ear (in headphones). In the app the drone keeps sounding for as long as you like; these samples are 4 seconds (the scale ones 11) and fade out.

  • All four are exactly in tune: the tuner's own pitch detector reads the root within 0.12 cents.
  • The fifth is tuned pure (a hair, 2 cents, above a piano's), so the drone itself never wobbles.
  • On the phone's speaker the cello and the organ sound much quieter and thinner than in headphones: most of their sound is too low for a small speaker. The tanpura hardly changes.
  • The guitar is synthetic (the app's own reference tones), so judge the drone, not the guitar.
